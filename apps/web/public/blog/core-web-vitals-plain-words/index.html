Core Web Vitals in plain words for site owners

LCP, INP and CLS without the jargon: what a visitor feels, why field and lab data disagree, and which fixes belong to content and which to developers.

Published and checked: November 13, 2026

A performance report often reads like a row of acronyms: LCP, INP, CLS. For a site owner they stand for three simple things a visitor notices: did the main content show up quickly, does the site respond when I tap, and does the page jump around under my finger. This article explains the metrics without jargon, shows why different tools report different numbers, and helps you split the work between the people who publish content and the developers who maintain the site.

Three questions a visitor asks

Core Web Vitals are the metrics Google uses to describe the real experience of a page. There are three of them today, and each one answers a single question.

Metric What the visitor notices Good threshold
LCP (Largest Contentful Paint) Has the main content appeared: the hero, the headline, the product photo 2.5 seconds or less
INP (Interaction to Next Paint) Does the site react to a click, a tap or a key press 200 milliseconds or less
CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift) Does content stay put while I read or aim for a button 0.1 or less

The thresholds are measured at the 75th percentile of page visits, separately for mobile and desktop. In plain terms, at least three out of four visits should fall inside the limit. A page passes only when all three metrics are good. CLS is not measured in seconds: it is a score that reflects how far content moved and how much of the screen it affected.

INP counts mouse clicks, taps on a touchscreen and key presses. Scrolling, hovering and zooming are not part of it. Anything above 500 milliseconds is rated poor: a visitor taps “Add to cart” and clearly waits before anything changes on screen.

Field data and lab data: why the numbers differ

Field data comes from real visitors. The public source is the Chrome User Experience Report (CrUX), anonymised statistics from Chrome users that also feed PageSpeed Insights and the Core Web Vitals report in Search Console. It tells you what actually happened, but it does not update instantly and may be missing entirely for a site with few visits.

Lab data comes from running a tool such as Lighthouse under fixed conditions: one device profile, one network, one page. The lab is good for finding a cause and checking a fix before release. It cannot see real taps, though, so it cannot measure INP. Lighthouse reports Total Blocking Time instead, an indirect measure of how long scripts keep the browser busy.

  • Field poor, lab good. Real visitors may have slower phones or networks than the test assumed, or the problem only appears after an interaction.
  • Lab poor, field good. The test may have been harsher than real conditions. The cause is still worth understanding, but it is not an emergency.
  • No field data. That is not a zero and not a failure, just too small a sample. Say so in the report and rely on repeated lab runs.

You need both. The field tells you whether people have a problem; the lab tells you where to look.

What the content team can often fix

Some performance problems come from what gets published rather than from code. Here a site owner or editor can act without a developer.

  • Image size. A multi-megabyte photo straight from a camera in the hero slows down the main content. Upload images close to the size they are displayed at, and compress them before publishing if your CMS does not do it for you.
  • Heavy blocks above the fold. A five-slide carousel, a video and several widgets at the top of the page all compete for the same connection. Keep what helps a visitor understand the offer and move the rest lower.
  • Embeds and banners. A video, a map, an ad slot or an announcement that appears above the text often pushes the page down. Before adding one, ask whether the template reserves space for it.
  • Third-party widgets. Every chat, counter or social button adds a script. Review regularly which ones the business still needs.

Content fixes do not replace technical work, but they often remove the most visible problems on specific pages, and you can make them today.

What usually belongs to a developer

Other causes live in the template, the server or the scripts. Knowing what a delay is made of helps you write a clear task.

LCP. web.dev breaks it into four parts: the time to the first byte from the server, the delay before the main resource starts loading, how long that resource takes to load, and the delay before it is rendered. A slow server, an image discovered late and render-blocking styles or scripts each need a different fix. Do not lazy-load the main image: web.dev says plainly never to lazy-load the LCP image, because the browser then starts fetching it later. fetchpriority="high" can help when it is set on one or two images that really matter, not on all of them.

INP. A slow response usually means the browser is busy running JavaScript at the moment of the tap. A developer looks for long tasks, heavy event handlers and third-party scripts competing with the interface.

CLS. Images and video need width and height attributes or space reserved in CSS. The template should hold room for ads, embeds and banners in advance. Web fonts can also shift text when the fallback font is swapped for the real one; this is tuned through how fonts load and a fallback font with similar proportions.

How to brief the work and check the result

A good task describes a situation, not just a metric: “On mobile, the main category photo appears late”, or “The enquiry button moves when the consent banner appears”. Add the address, the device, the data source and the date of the measurement.

  1. Pick representatives of your key templates: home page, category, product or service page, enquiry page.
  2. Record the current field and lab values, separately for mobile and desktop.
  3. Ship one group of changes and repeat the lab runs on the same addresses under the same conditions.
  4. Check field data later: it accumulates over a period, so a release does not show up in it straight away.
  5. Make sure forms, menus and the consent banner still work after the optimisation.

In FluxRadar, the Complete audit has a dedicated performance section, and its reliability checks include a slow server response rule: it flags a page whose response took longer than 1.8 seconds to arrive during the crawl. The knowledge-base guide to reading performance data (in Ukrainian) explains that section.

Good Core Web Vitals make a page more pleasant to use, but they do not guarantee search positions or sales. They are one sign of a healthy site, not a replacement for useful content.
