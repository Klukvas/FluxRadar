Internal links that get important pages found

Orphan pages, click depth, descriptive anchor text and JavaScript-only menus: how to find the important pages on your site that nothing links to.

Published and checked: November 13, 2026

An important page can be well written, carry a proper title and sit in the sitemap, yet stay almost invisible. The reason is often simple: nothing links to it, or reaching it takes five levels of menus. Internal links are the paths that both visitors and search crawlers use to move through a site. This article looks at how those paths break and how to find the pages nobody can reach.

Links are the map of your site for people and crawlers

A search crawler finds new pages much like a visitor does: by following links. Google’s documentation is direct about it: every page you care about should have a link from at least one other page on your site.

A sitemap helps you tell a search engine about your addresses, but it does not guarantee that they will all be crawled and indexed. Google notes that a small site (about 500 pages or fewer meant for search) that is comprehensively linked internally may not need a sitemap at all. Links are the foundation; the sitemap is a safety net.

For a business this is also about sales. If a service page can only be reached from an ad, a visitor who lands on the home page will never find it.

Orphan pages and pages held by one link

An orphan page is a page that no internal link points to. Common causes:

  • the page was built for a campaign and never added to a menu or section;
  • a redesign removed the block that used to link to it;
  • a product or article still exists in the CMS, but the section that listed it was hidden;
  • links still point to an old address while the new page lives on its own.

A weaker version of the same problem is a page held by a single link. While that link exists, all is well. Remove one banner or rewrite one paragraph, and the page becomes an orphan.

FluxRadar has two checks for this. The first flags a page that the sitemap lists and the crawl read successfully, but that no crawled page links to. If the sitemap could not be read or the crawl did not collect the full link graph, the rule draws no conclusion, so a page is not called an orphan just because its only source was left out of the crawl. The second check flags a page that exactly one other page links to and names that source.

Finding an orphan does not always mean you should rush to add a link. First decide whether the page is still needed. If it is current, link to it from the section it logically belongs to. If it is outdated, update it, merge it with another page, or remove it from the sitemap and set up a proper server response. A page you are not ready to show visitors in the site structure probably does not belong in the sitemap either.

Click depth: how many steps to reach a page

Click depth is the number of link steps from the home page or another entry point to a page. The deeper a page sits, the fewer people reach it and the longer crawlers may take to find it.

Depth on its own is not an error: a large catalogue will always have deep pages. What matters is that the pages that bring enquiries or answer customers’ main questions do not end up buried.

FluxRadar measures this distance from the crawl’s entry page by following links, and flags pages that are four or more steps away. A page that cannot be reached through links at all does not get this finding: it is the orphan case from the previous section.

Ways to reduce depth without overloading the menu:

  • add hub pages for the main sections that link to the key subsections;
  • use breadcrumbs so every page links back up the structure;
  • link from popular articles to the related services or products;
  • make catalogue pagination plain links, so later pages of a list stay reachable.

Anchor text that means something

Anchor text, the visible words of a link, tells both people and search engines what to expect on the other side. Google recommends that it be descriptive, reasonably concise and relevant to the page it sits on. A simple test: read only the link text, without the sentence around it. Is it clear where it goes?

For an image link, the image’s alt text acts as the anchor text, so fill it in meaningfully. At the same time, do not turn anchors into strings of keywords: that hurts reading and adds nothing.

Navigation that only exists in JavaScript

Google reliably recognises a link when it is an <a> element with an href attribute that resolves to a real address. A link inserted by a script is fine too, as long as it uses the same markup. Trouble starts when navigation is built another way:

  • a <span> or a button that opens a page through a click handler;
  • an <a> with no href, or with an href that runs a script instead of pointing to an address;
  • a menu or product list that only appears after scrolling or clicking and has no addresses of its own.

A person can use such navigation without trouble, while a crawler may see no path at all. A developer only needs to confirm that the main menu, breadcrumbs and pagination are plain links. Our own case study on making FluxRadar readable without JavaScript (in Ukrainian) shows what this looks like in practice.

How to find important pages that nothing links to

Start with a list of the pages that earn money or answer customers’ frequent questions: services, key categories, contacts, prices, delivery terms. Then:

  1. Confirm these addresses are in the sitemap and open with status 200.
  2. Crawl the site from the home page and compare the pages found through links with your list.
  3. For each page on the list, check how many other pages link to it and how deep it sits.
  4. Add links from sections, hubs and related content, not only from the footer.
  5. Crawl again and confirm the page is now reachable.

Do not try to solve everything with a footer of a hundred links or an “all pages” block. Such lists are hard to read and do not explain how pages relate to each other. A link in the text that answers the reader’s next question does more: from an article on choosing an air conditioner to the installation page, from a category to the delivery terms.

Internal links do not guarantee indexing or rankings, but without them even the best page is a room with no door.
