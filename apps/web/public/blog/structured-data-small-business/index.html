Structured data for a small business: what is worth adding

A short list of the markup types that genuinely help a small company, and an honest note on what not to expect from them.

Published and checked: November 19, 2026

Structured data describes a page in a form machines can read easily: the company name, its address and opening hours, a product's price, where the page sits in the site. It is usually added as JSON-LD, a separate block of code in the HTML that visitors never see. For the owner of a small business, the real question is not how to write it but which parts are worth doing at all. Below are the types that usually make sense, and the caveats that will save your developer time.

What to expect from markup

Google uses structured data to understand what a page contains and, for some types, to show a richer result: a logo, a breadcrumb trail, a price. Its general guidelines are explicit, though: even correct markup does not guarantee that a rich result will appear. Treat markup as a precise description of what is already on the page, not as a lever for better rankings.

One rule matters more than any choice of type: markup must describe visible content. Google asks site owners not to mark up content that readers cannot see, and not to mark up irrelevant or misleading content such as fake reviews. Breaking these rules can lead to a manual action, after which the page is no longer eligible for rich results.

Of the three supported formats, Google recommends JSON-LD. It is easy to add to a site template and to check separately from the page layout.

Organization: who is behind the site

Organization markup describes the company as a whole. According to Google's documentation, some of its properties help tell your organization apart from others with a similar name, and some can influence how the company appears in search, such as the logo.

No properties are required. Google recommends placing the markup on the home page or on a single page that describes the organization. A sensible starting set:

  • name: the name as it is written on the site;
  • url: the address of the home page;
  • logo: a link to the logo file;
  • sameAs: the company's official profiles on other sites, if it has any;
  • contactPoint: the main way to get in touch, if someone actually answers it.

There is no need to repeat Organization on every page with slightly different details. One consistent description is more reliable than ten similar ones.

LocalBusiness: if customers come to you

A café, salon, garage or clinic gets more from LocalBusiness markup. Google requires two properties: name and address, the physical address with as much detail as possible. The recommended ones are telephone, openingHoursSpecification for opening hours, geo for coordinates, url and priceRange.

Pick the most specific subtype you can; Google gives Restaurant, DaySpa and HealthClub as examples. The generic LocalBusiness type fits only when nothing more precise does.

Here is an illustrative example for a made-up café. The address, phone number and hours are placeholders; your markup must carry exactly the details written on your contact page: 

{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "CafeOrCoffeeShop",
  "name": "Example Roasters",
  "url": "https://example.com/",
  "telephone": "+44 0000 000000",
  "address": {
    "@type": "PostalAddress",
    "streetAddress": "1 Example Street",
    "addressLocality": "Leeds",
    "addressCountry": "GB"
  },
  "openingHoursSpecification": [{
    "@type": "OpeningHoursSpecification",
    "dayOfWeek": ["Monday", "Tuesday", "Wednesday", "Thursday", "Friday"],
    "opens": "08:00",
    "closes": "20:00"
  }]
}

The most common problem here is drift. The contact page shows the new hours while the markup still has last year's; the header lists one phone number and the JSON-LD another. Before publishing, compare the markup with what visitors see and with the business profile you keep on external services.

Product and breadcrumbs: for shops and catalogues

If you sell online, Product markup on product pages deserves the most attention. Google separates two cases: pages where customers can buy the product (merchant listings) and pages where they cannot, such as reviews (product snippets). Each has its own set of properties, so your developer should follow the documentation for your case.

The price, currency and availability in the markup must match what the shopper sees. If the template reads the visible price from one source and the markup price from another, they will drift apart at the first price change.

BreadcrumbList describes the path to a page: Home → Category → Product. It is easy to add to a template and useful for sites with several levels. Note the limit: since January 2025, Google shows breadcrumbs in search results on desktop only, not on mobile.

Type For whom Keep in mind
Organization Any company One description, on the home or About page
LocalBusiness A business with a physical address name and address required; most specific subtype
Product An online shop Price and availability as shown on the page
BreadcrumbList A site with categories Shown in search on desktop only

FAQ and reviews: where expectations meet reality

A lot of advice still recommends FAQPage markup to take up more room in search results. That no longer works. According to Google Search's documentation changelog, the FAQ rich result stopped appearing from May 7, 2026, and its documentation was removed in June 2026. A page that answers real customer questions is still useful to people; just do not expect it to look different in search.

Reviews follow a similar pattern. In the LocalBusiness documentation, review and aggregateRating are recommended only for sites that capture reviews about other local businesses. If a company marks up reviews of itself on its own site, it should not count on stars in Google. Show genuine reviews to visitors, but never invent ratings or add them to markup to chase stars.

The practical order is short: start with Organization; add LocalBusiness if customers visit an address, Product if you run a shop, and BreadcrumbList if you have categories. Add anything else only when you know why.

What FluxRadar checks

In the paid Basic and Complete plans, the SEO module reads the JSON-LD blocks on crawled pages and reports:

  • a block that cannot be parsed as JSON, since one stray comma breaks the whole block;
  • an object without @context or without @type; types declared on the nodes of an @graph count as present;
  • a page with a visible address and phone number but no LocalBusiness or recognized subtype in its markup, reported as an opportunity rather than an error;
  • a local business description missing name or an address, or the recommended phone, opening hours or coordinates;
  • review or rating markup on a same-site LocalBusiness, as a reminder of the rules for stars.

The limits matter too. FluxRadar reads markup from the HTML the server returns, so JSON-LD that a script adds after the page loads may not be seen. It does not judge whether a page is eligible for a rich result, and it does not compare prices or hours in the markup with the visible text. Use Google's Rich Results Test for that, and look at a few pages yourself.
