Page titles and descriptions people want to click

The title and description are the first things people see of your site in search. How to make them clear for every type of page, and why Google sometimes shows different text.

Published and checked: November 19, 2026

In search results, a page is represented by two lines: a linked title and a short description below it. That is what people use to decide whether to open your site. Both usually come from the HTML: the title from the <title> element, the description from the page text or from <meta name="description">. On a small site they are easy to write by hand. Trouble starts when there are hundreds of pages and a template writes the titles. Here is how to handle that systematically.

One template, one title rule

Google advises giving each page its own descriptive, concise title. The easiest way to get there is to agree a rule for each type of page instead of writing titles one at a time. An illustrative example for a small shop:

Page type Rule Example
Home Name + what you do Example Roasters: freshly roasted coffee in Leeds
Category Category name + qualifier Espresso beans | Example Roasters
Product Product name + key detail Ethiopia Yirgacheffe, 250 g | Example Roasters
Article What it is about, in plain words How to choose a grind for a moka pot

Google suggests you may put the site name at the start or the end of the title. What matters more: do not repeat the same words several times, and do not turn the title into a list of search terms.

One more simple check: the title, the main <h1> heading and the first paragraph should be about the same thing. They do not have to match word for word; the title can be shorter and carry the company name. But if the title says "Flower delivery" and the page is about wedding decoration, the visitor will feel they landed in the wrong place and will probably go back to the results. A mismatch between them is also one reason search may show a different title, as covered below.

Length: truncation, not a limit

Google's documentation says there is no limit on how long a <title> can be. In search results, though, the title link is truncated as needed, usually to fit the width of the device. Descriptions work the same way: there is no maximum length, but the snippet is shortened in results.

The practical rule follows: put the most important words first. If a title opens with the company name and a slogan and leaves the product name for the end, the product name is what disappears on a phone. Figures such as "up to 60 characters" that appear in advice are an editor's guide, not a Google requirement.

When Google shows a different title

Google generates title links automatically. It considers not only <title> but also the main heading (<h1>), og:title, the text of links pointing to the page and other sources. Text other than your title most often appears when:

  • the title is empty or part of it is missing;
  • the title is obsolete, for example a page reused every year still shows last year;
  • the title does not reflect what the page is about;
  • a group of pages shares the same boilerplate text in their titles;
  • the language or writing system of the title does not match the page's main content.

Google says it cannot change title links by hand for individual sites. So when results show something other than what you wrote, treat it as a signal: check that the title really describes the page and is not repeated on its neighbours.

The description: a hint, not a guarantee

Google mostly builds the snippet from the page content itself, and uses the meta description when it describes the page more accurately. So write the description as if it will appear, but do not be surprised when different queries show different parts of the page.

What Google recommends:

  • make each description unique; identical descriptions across a site do not help when individual pages appear in search;
  • include useful details such as a price, author or date that might not otherwise make it into the snippet;
  • do not write the description as a list of keywords;
  • on large, database-driven sites, generate descriptions programmatically, but make sure they read naturally and differ from page to page.

An illustrative example for a product page: "Ethiopia Yirgacheffe, 250 g, roasted fresh every Monday. Floral and citrus notes, suits filter and espresso. Delivery across the UK." It carries what helps someone choose, with no repeated category name.

To keep part of a page out of the snippet, use the data-nosnippet attribute; the max-snippet rule caps the snippet length, and nosnippet turns it off entirely.

Duplicates in a catalogue

Identical titles most often show up in catalogues: paginated pages, colour variants of a product, filters, tag archives on a blog. The template inserts the category name, and ten addresses end up with the same title and description. Neither a search engine nor a person looking at results can tell them apart.

The fix depends on whether the pages really are different:

  • if they are different pages, add what sets them apart to the template: the page number, the colour, the filter name, the city;
  • if they are copies of one page, choose the main address and point the copies at it with <link rel="canonical">;
  • if a page should not be in search at all, decide that separately, not through its title.

You do not need to open pages one by one to find duplicates. Export a list of addresses and titles from your site's admin or from a crawler report, sort it by title and look for repeated rows. Start with the largest groups: there is usually a single template rule behind them, and one fix covers dozens of pages.

After changing a template, check several pages of each type, not just one: a mistake in the rule usually affects the whole group.

What FluxRadar checks

The free check looks at the home page; the Basic and Complete plans look at every crawled page. A finding appears when:

  • <title> is missing or empty, shorter than 10 or longer than 70 characters;
  • the meta description is missing or empty, shorter than 50 or longer than 160 characters;
  • in the paid plans, several crawled pages share the same title or the same description and none of them points to another with a canonical tag.

The length bounds are FluxRadar's review thresholds, not a Google rule; as above, Google sets no hard limit. The tool does not judge whether a title is appealing, and it does not know which text Google actually shows. To see the text search actually shows, look up a few of your pages in Google yourself.
